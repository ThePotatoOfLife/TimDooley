# European Sector Atlas — Reading Guide

The sector atlas is the missing middle layer between the repository's broad World nodes and the detailed European Economic Graph.

A country is not an economy. An economy is not a sector. A sector is not a company. A company is not an isolated entity. The useful object is the chain connecting them.

For every sector, the repository should eventually be able to answer:

1. **Inputs** — What does the sector require?
2. **Assets** — What physical, intellectual and financial assets make production possible?
3. **Workers** — Which skills and occupations are necessary?
4. **Infrastructure** — Which ports, grids, roads, cables, laboratories, buildings or data systems are required?
5. **Ownership** — Who owns the productive assets?
6. **Control** — Who can actually interrupt, regulate or redirect the system?
7. **Finance** — Who lends, invests, insures or guarantees it?
8. **Procurement** — Who buys its output, especially the public sector?
9. **Trade** — What crosses borders and through which corridors?
10. **Technology** — Which software, machinery, chips and intellectual property are upstream?
11. **Energy** — How much energy is required and at what point in the chain?
12. **Water/materials** — Which physical resources constrain it?
13. **Geography** — Where are the bottlenecks and concentrations?
14. **Regulation** — Which institutions determine what can be produced and sold?
15. **Labour** — Where are the skills located, and where are shortages emerging?
16. **Resilience** — What happens if one supplier, port, cable, fuel or market disappears?
17. **Outputs** — Which downstream sectors depend upon it?
18. **Repair** — What maintenance, recycling, replacement and renewal keep it alive?

## The first major discovery

The repository previously had many of these concepts as separate nouns: energy, infrastructure, industry, labour, technology, research, trade, ownership and obligations. The sector atlas makes them **interdependent**.

For example:

**Semiconductors** require specialised chemicals, ultrapure water, electricity, equipment, advanced materials, software and highly trained workers.

Those chips then enter automobiles, industrial machines, telecom networks, defence systems, medical devices, electricity controls and data centres.

A semiconductor dependency therefore cannot be understood by looking at the semiconductor company alone. It is a dependency graph.

The same logic applies to transformers, pharmaceuticals, batteries, ports, subsea cables, cloud compute, fertiliser, machine tools and industrial software.

## The physical economy beneath the digital economy

The repository should resist the temptation to make the technology layer weightless.

Cloud requires buildings, fibre, electricity, cooling, chips, land and capital.

AI requires compute, compute requires chips, chips require fabrication equipment and materials, fabrication requires power and water, and all of it requires finance and specialised labour.

The digital layer is therefore coupled to the physical layer.

## Housing and food are not peripheral

European Environment Agency analysis identifies housing and food as major material-footprint hotspots. In the analysed 2021 data they together represented 72% of the EU material footprint. That makes housing and food central nodes in the material economy rather than merely consumer-side topics.

This creates additional graph branches:

**Housing → construction → cement/steel/timber → energy → finance → land → labour → transport → household wealth**

and:

**Food → agriculture → land/water/fertiliser → machinery → processing → cold chain → logistics → retail → households**

These are examples of how an apparently ordinary sector becomes a large systems graph.

## Sector coupling is a core research object

Energy prices propagate through agriculture, distribution and transport and then through broader production and services. Water is simultaneously an agricultural input, an industrial input, an electricity-system input and a public-service system. Ports can simultaneously be trade nodes, energy nodes, industrial nodes and security nodes.

The atlas should therefore prefer relationships such as:

`sector A → requires → infrastructure B`

`sector A → depends-on → material C`

`sector A → employs → skill D`

`sector A → financed-by → institution E`

`sector A → supplies → sector F`

rather than isolated sector descriptions.

## Strategic dependency is more precise than "self-sufficiency"

The aim is not to assume that Europe should produce everything itself.

A dependency can be acceptable when it is diversified, substitutable, stockpiled, contractually secure and connected to reliable partners. It becomes more strategically important when a single external source controls a large share of a critical input and replacement would take years.

This distinction should be recorded as:

**dependency → concentration → substitutability → lead time → consequence → mitigation**

rather than as a simple domestic/foreign binary.

## The North Programme connection

The North Programme can now use the sector atlas as its empirical middle layer.

The sequence becomes:

**North Axis → geography → states → institutions → sectors → firms → assets → infrastructure → inputs → outputs → dependencies → obligations → repair.**

That is the bridge from the mythic tree to the observable economic graph.

The symbolic architecture remains symbolic. The empirical graph remains evidence-based. The connection between them is methodological: both ask what is connected to what, where the connections carry life, and where broken connections create fragility.

## Research order

The highest-value next datasets are not necessarily the largest industries. They are the sectors that sit at the intersection of many others:

- electricity grids and transformers;
- ports and maritime logistics;
- critical raw materials and processing;
- semiconductors and electronics;
- pharmaceuticals and active ingredients;
- cloud, data centres and AI compute;
- industrial software;
- machine tools;
- water systems;
- food processing and cold chains;
- construction materials;
- public procurement;
- pensions, insurance and infrastructure ownership;
- skilled technical labour;
- subsea cables and repair capacity.

These are high-connectivity nodes. Mapping them can illuminate large portions of the system at once.
