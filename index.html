OF LIFE

You found the patch. Sit here a minute. A person, a potato, a philosophy, a religion, a public life and a very large piece of the internet all grew into this place.

This is home for Tim Dooley & The Potato of Life. It grew out of years spent in public on the internet: livestreams, arguments, jokes, writing, songs, religious language, strange encounters, research, code, maps, mistakes, revisions and a potato that refused to remain only a joke.

Tim is a Danish writer, livestreamer, storyteller and archive-builder. The Potato of Life became the central image of a larger project called Potatoism: a developing philosophy, religion and culture about growth, conditions, freedom, power, repair, nourishment and what a life leaves behind. You do not have to believe any of it to look around.

Some people arrive because they know Tim. Some arrive because somebody said “God.” Some saw a Potato, heard a song, found an argument, followed a Bible comparison, or simply clicked a strange link. You can begin from whichever version of the story actually brought you here.

What are we doing here?

Trying to grow something that can survive contact with reality

The project is not only collecting Tim. It is trying to turn a long, chaotic public life into memory, ideas, works and tools that another person can actually inspect and use.

RememberKeep the public life larger than a clip.

Years of internet life are easy to flatten into one quote, one accusation, one title or one mythology. Story, Timeline and sources keep development visible: what happened, what Tim said then, what he says now, and what remains uncertain.

Story → Timeline →
GrowTurn the Potato into something people can use.

Potatoism asks whether growth can produce more capable, truthful and independent people rather than more obedient followers. The philosophy is allowed to leave Tim, be criticized, be improved and fail its own Fruit test.

Potatoism → Practice →
MakeLet the ideas become books, music, images, games and code.

The Great Book, albums, TimAmp, maps and the repository are not decoration around the philosophy. They are places where the ideas have to become something concrete enough to work, break, delight somebody or teach us something we did not know before making it.

Works → Music →
TestKeep mythology and evidence close enough to argue.

Religion can compare texts. Philosophy can question premises. Science can reject a model. World can impose material constraints. Sources can correct memory. The archive is strongest when those rooms disagree productively instead of all becoming proof of the same conclusion.

Science → Evidence →
ReturnPut something useful back into the world.

The project keeps returning to one stubborn standard: Fruit. Does a claim, role, archive, relationship or system produce capability, nourishment, clarity, repair, art, protection or better future Seed—or only more attention around itself?

Philosophy → World →

That is the larger experiment: can a life lived noisily on the internet become a Garden instead of only an archive of noise?

Why an archive this large?

Because a public life becomes easy to flatten

Fifteen years of streams, arguments, jokes, beliefs, songs, mistakes, changing identities and creative work can be reduced to one clip, one label or one mythology. This site tries to preserve enough context that a reader can inspect development rather than inherit a summary.

Read the person across time

Old statements can disagree with newer ones. The Timeline and Story preserve change instead of manufacturing a timeless persona.

Timeline →
Separate claim from evidence

Autobiography, theology, memory, public artifacts, outside research and interpretation do not automatically prove one another.

Sources & evidence →
Let ideas leave Tim

Potatoism becomes more interesting when a reader can use, criticize or reject individual ideas without becoming loyal to the founder.

Potatoism →
Let works speak in their own media

Books, music, games, images, maps and software can reveal things prose misses—and can fail in ways prose hides.
