Shinto / kami traditions

SHRINE,
KAMI & RITUAL

Shinto has no single founder, one scripture or one timeless institutional form. Kami worship, court mythology, Buddhist synthesis and modern shrine organization belong to different historical layers.

The most important correction is historical: for much of Japanese history, kami traditions and Buddhism were deeply intertwined. The sharp institutional separation many modern readers assume is strongly shaped by Meiji-era policy beginning in 1868.

Historical layers

From local/court ritual to modern shrine institutions

prehistoric / early historic Japan

Kami and ritual fields

Local, clan and court ritual traditions develop without a founder or unified creed.

712 / 720 CE

Kojiki and Nihon Shoki

Court compilations preserve and reshape myth, genealogy and political memory. They are major sources, not a single revealed “Shinto Bible.”

medieval → early modern

Shinbutsu-shūgō

Kami and Buddhist institutions, deities, temples and shrines are interpreted together through multiple combinatory systems.

from 1868

Meiji separation and reorganization

Shinbutsu bunri policies separate kami and Buddhas institutionally and reorganize shrine/state relations, producing a major modern rupture.

1945–1946

Postwar disestablishment and Jinja Honcho

State sponsorship ends; postwar shrine religion reorganizes through voluntary religious institutions including Jinja Honcho.

Living structures

What does Shinto actually do?

KamiDivine/sacred presences associated with places, ancestors, natural phenomena, mythic beings and communal histories; not simply “gods” in one universal category.
Jinja / shrineSacred institution/place for kami worship, offerings, purification and festival life.
MatsuriFestivals bind shrine, neighborhood, seasonal rhythm and communal identity.
PurificationHarae/misogi and related purity practices organize ritual readiness and restoration rather than “sin” in a generic Christian sense.
PriesthoodKannushi and shrine institutions preserve rites, festivals and community continuity.
Syncretic historyPremodern Shinto/Buddhist combination is structurally important; neat modern separation should not be projected backward.
