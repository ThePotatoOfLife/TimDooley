DENOMINATIONS

Ancient religious worlds are better understood through cities, temples, cults, ritual specialists, calendars, texts, archaeology and changing political orders than through modern church-style family trees.

A myth is not the whole religion. A god list is not the whole religion. A funerary book is not the whole religion. This reader starts instead with what people did, where they did it, which institutions preserved it, and which texts or objects survive.

Dates below are period markers. Ancient traditions normally have no founder birthday, and their surviving texts may be much later than the practices or oral traditions they preserve.

Ancient Egypt

Temple, kingship, maat and the route through death

Old KingdomMiddle KingdomNew KingdomLate / Ptolemaic / Roman
Old KingdomPyramid Texts: royal funerary inscriptions and ritual texts, especially in 5th–6th Dynasty pyramids.
First Intermediate / Middle KingdomCoffin Texts: funerary traditions broaden onto coffins; the Book of Two Ways preserves an extraordinary mapped afterlife cosmography.
New Kingdom onwardBook of Going Forth by Day / Book of the Dead: variable collections of spells and instructions, not one fixed Egyptian Bible.
MaatTruth, order, balance and right arrangement—both a divine figure and a principle that constrains kingship and judgment.
Osiris / judgmentDeath, renewal, justified status and judgment traditions grow over time; the weighing-of-the-heart scene belongs to this larger funerary development.
Temple cultDaily cult, priesthood, processions and regional deity traditions matter as much as funerary literature.

Mesopotamia

City, divine house, king and omen

Sumerian citiesAkkadianBabylonianAssyrianlate temple survivals

Mesopotamian religion lasts for more than three millennia and changes with city, language, dynasty and empire. The temple is repeatedly understood as the house of the deity, with cult image, offerings, priestly service and city identity forming one religious-political ecology.

City god / divine houseUruk, Nippur, Babylon, Assur and other centers organize divine power differently. A city and its principal deity can be institutionally intertwined.
DivinationOmens, celestial observation, extispicy and specialist interpretation make divine communication a technical institutional practice.
UnderworldInanna/Ishtar descent, Ereshkigal, Nergal and funerary/lament traditions belong to changing textual worlds rather than one fixed doctrine.

Ancient Greece

Sanctuary, sacrifice, festival, oracle and mystery

ArchaicClassicalHellenisticRoman-period Greek cults

Homer and Hesiod profoundly shape Greek mythic imagination, but Greek religion is lived through sanctuary, sacrifice, festival, local cult, oracle, household rites and initiation. The Greeks have no single scripture comparable to the Bible or Qur'an.

Polis cultCities organize calendars, priesthoods, sanctuaries and patron deities. Athena at Athens is not merely a mythic character; cult and civic identity meet.
OracleDelphi and other oracular centers make divination and sacred consultation part of interstate and personal decision-making.
MysteriesEleusinian, Dionysian/Orphic and other initiatory traditions are diverse and locally specific; secrecy and afterlife hope do not make them one mystery religion.

Ancient Rome

Civic ritual, household gods and an expanding pantheon

RepublicAugustan settlementImperial cultslate-antique transformation

Roman religio centers strongly on correct ritual relations with the gods. Public sacrifice, priestly offices, augury, household shrines and imported cults coexist. Greek identifications influence Roman religion, but Rome is not simply Greece translated into Latin.

Household cultLares, Penates and domestic shrines place religious maintenance inside ordinary household space.
Civic / priestly religionPriestly colleges, sacrifice, calendars, vows and divination support the welfare and continuity of the civic community.
Imperial / imported cultsDeified rulers and cults such as Cybele, Isis and Mithras belong to different institutional histories inside an unusually absorptive imperial religious field.
