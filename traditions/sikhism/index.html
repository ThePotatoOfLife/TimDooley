Sikh tradition

GURU,
GRANTH & PANTH

Sikh history is carried through a succession of Gurus, scripture, institutions and community discipline—not by reducing Sikhism to a mixture of neighboring religions.

Guru Nanak's teaching in fifteenth- and sixteenth-century Punjab begins a Guru lineage that develops through ten human Gurus. Scripture, congregational institutions, the Harmandir Sahib, the Khalsa and the later enduring authority of Guru Granth Sahib become distinct historical layers.

Historical sequence

Major Sikh clocks

1469–1539
Guru Nanak

Teaching/community era of the first Sikh Guru and beginning of the historical Guru succession.

1604
Adi Granth compiled and installed

Guru Arjan compiles the scripture and installs it at Harmandir Sahib in Amritsar, establishing a major textual and institutional center.

1606 onward
Martyrdom, authority and community transformation

Guru Arjan's death and later Guru-period conflicts reshape Sikh political and communal institutions.

1699
Khalsa inaugurated at Anandpur Sahib

Guru Gobind Singh gives concrete form to the initiated Khalsa community, with discipline, identity and collective authority.

1708
End of human Guru succession

Sikh tradition locates enduring Guruship in the Guru Granth Sahib, while the Panth/community remains a major bearer of collective religious life.

What holds the tradition together?

Several forms of authority at once

Guru lineageTen human Gurus form the formative historical succession from Guru Nanak through Guru Gobind Singh.
Guru Granth SahibScriptural authority and living Guru status in Sikh tradition; compiled in major form under Guru Arjan and given final Guruship in the later Sikh tradition.
KhalsaInitiated disciplined community given concrete form in 1699; not equivalent to “all Sikhs” as a simple sociological label.
Gurdwara / sangatCongregational worship and community space centered on scripture, kirtan, prayer and collective life.
LangarShared communal meal embodying equality, service and hospitality as lived institution rather than abstract doctrine.
Miri / piriTemporal and spiritual dimensions of authority become important in Sikh political-religious development.
