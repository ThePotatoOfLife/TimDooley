Judaism develops through ancient Israelite religion, Temple-centered worlds, rabbinic reconstruction, legal interpretation, mystical currents and modern denominational identities.

Judaism is not best understood as a sequence of denominations. Its continuity is carried through peoplehood, scripture, law, ritual, memory, institutions and interpretation. Some later branches are denominations; others are schools, mystical currents or sister traditions.

Historical spine

Major clocks

c. 1200–586 BCE

Israelite / Judahite religious formation

Ancient Israelite religion develops within a broader ancient Near Eastern field rather than appearing in one founding event.

586 BCE

First Temple destruction and exile rupture

A major political and religious discontinuity that helps reshape scripture, identity and portable religious life.

539–332 BCE

Post-exilic / Persian-period reorganization

Temple, Torah, priesthood and communal forms reorganize over a long period.

c. 516 BCE–70 CE

Second Temple Jewish field

A plural field including different priestly, sectarian, apocalyptic and interpretive communities.

70 CE

Second Temple destruction

A major rupture after which rabbinic Judaism becomes one central surviving and developing Jewish trajectory.

c. 200 / c. 500 CE

Mishnah and Babylonian Talmud consolidation

Rabbinic law, debate and interpretation take durable textual forms over centuries.

Branches and currents

Not everything here is the same kind of branch

Rabbinic JudaismThe main post-Temple trunk behind most later Jewish communal forms; grounded in Torah plus rabbinic interpretation and halakhic development.trunk tradition
Samaritan Israelite traditionAn ancient sister lineage rooted in Israelite tradition, centered on its own Pentateuchal tradition and Mount Gerizim; not a denomination that split from modern Judaism.sister Israelite lineage
Karaite JudaismBecomes distinct especially in the 8th–9th centuries; rejects rabbinic Oral Torah authority in favor of direct scriptural interpretation in its own tradition.historical differentiation
KabbalahA mystical current, not a denomination. Medieval Kabbalah crystallizes especially in the 12th–13th centuries and later develops major Lurianic forms.mystical current
Hasidic Judaism18th-century revival/mystical movement within rabbinic Judaism, developing many dynasties and communities rather than one centralized denomination.revival / mystical movement
Modern Orthodox JudaismA modern denominational/self-identification that emerges through 19th-century differentiation; includes multiple internal trajectories including Haredi and Modern Orthodox worlds.modern denominational identity
Reform / Liberal JudaismModern reform movement with strong early institutional markers in Hamburg in 1817–1818 and later international development.modern reform movement
Conservative / Masorti JudaismModern movement balancing historical-critical scholarship, rabbinic tradition and evolving halakhic interpretation; 1886 JTS is a major U.S. institutional marker.modern denomination / movement
Reconstructionist and other modern currentsLater modern Jewish movements should be placed as distinct intellectual/institutional developments rather than retrojected into ancient categories.modern movement family
