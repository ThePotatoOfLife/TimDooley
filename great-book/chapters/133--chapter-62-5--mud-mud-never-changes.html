Chapter 62.5

"Mud. Mud never changes."

"In the Potatoverse, mud is as old as history itself. The Romans waged wars over fields and soil, conquering lands and binding the roots of countless civilizations to their will. Spain’s empire rose from the lust to cultivate foreign lands, to take and till, leaving nothing but the faintest memory of growth in their wake. Hitler sought to reshape a world, feeding it visions of grandeur while drawing it ever deeper into the mud. But mud… mud never changes.

For centuries, we’ve waged wars over earth and riches, fields and crops. We’ve fought for power, land, and pride, and even in the name of belief itself. Blood has stained the soil in pursuit of everything from divinity to justice, from freedom to rage. And in each of those battles, the mud watched patiently, as all things returned to its quiet, unchanging depths.

In the year 1945, my great-great-grandfather, rooted in the fields of his homeland, wondered if he would see his family again, if the soil would welcome him back. He got his wish when the world learned of the atom, a force as old as the roots of life itself. Armageddon felt as close as the dirt beneath our feet, and the world braced itself for ruin. But then, miraculously, we saw the atom not as a weapon but as a near-limitless power, something to cultivate, to harvest. People prospered in ways once confined to the realms of dreams, living with machines, fusion-powered tools, and soil that yielded far more than it took. But as we grew complacent in that prosperity, the time came when we woke from the dream, when consumption led to scarcity. The world began to crumble. Peace, like a fertile field left untended, withered and became just another memory buried beneath layers of mud.

The year is now 2077, and we stand on the edge of ruin. Our crops have dwindled, our lands dried and cracked, the last fields of plenty on the brink of collapse. My roots are bound to this place, and I am afraid. Afraid for the ones I cherish, for the young sprouts who may never grow, and for a world that clings to the edge of rot. Because if history has taught us one truth, it’s that mud… mud never changes.

When atomic fire consumed the earth, those who survived burrowed deep, taking shelter in dark vaults beneath the soil, hidden from the light. When they emerged, the fields were gone, the landscape desolate. Those few who looked to the horizon found only a wasteland waiting to swallow them whole. In the west, the lights of New Vegas flickered on as if the fall had never come, preserved by the strange overseer Mr. House, who bound the land’s wealth to himself like roots tangled in the mud. Yet across the Colorado, a different tribe was rising. Caesar’s Legion, forged from 86 tribes broken and scattered like dried soil, began their march, gathering strength, preparing to claim what remained of the land.

Warriors battled for the last precious resources, a struggle to dominate what little soil was still fertile. For years, the New California Republic held the Hoover Dam, barely keeping the Legion at bay. But the Legion did not retreat; it bided its time, gathering strength, its forces growing like weeds in a fallow field, waiting to consume whatever dared to stand in their way.

War. War never changes.

As time passed, the land saw blood again, as we fought from Antietam to Amiens, Okinawa to Anchorage, believing each time that the fields we conquered would hold prosperity, that the soil would sustain our dreams. And each time, democracy, liberty, pride—all were slowly swallowed, faded back into the same mud that birthed them. And as we learned, mud cares little for ideals, little for pride or honor. It takes all things in time, leaving only whispers in the dirt where once stood cities and kings.

This is why, on this day—July 4th, 2076—three hundred years after the birth of a once-great nation, we gather to celebrate the completion of Vault 76. We have sought refuge in these vaults, fortresses beneath the soil engineered to withstand the mud’s claim, where we hope to rise again and till fields anew. It is a monument built by the hands of all who still remember what it was to till, to sow, to believe in green fields and growing things.

But as history has shown us, the mud never changes. It waits, it watches, it consumes.

In the end, it is not just war that remains the same. Every grand design, every vast empire, every fleeting hope—it all returns to the mud. We fight, we build, we plant our seeds, but it is the mud that will endure long after we are gone, unyielding, unchanging, as we return to its quiet embrace.

For in the Potatoverse, as in all worlds, the mud is eternal. And though we may rage against it, though we may rise and fall, toil and triumph, the mud, like a truth as old as the first potato, reminds us of one thing:

Mud… mud never changes."
