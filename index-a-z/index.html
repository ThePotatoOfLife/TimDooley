Words are doors, not conclusions

A–Z

Start with the word you know, then discover which world of meaning you are actually in.

Human language reuses successful words. “Root” can name plant anatomy, ancestry, mathematical solutions, a causal origin or project provenance. “Eye” can mean an organ, a potato bud, an old religious symbol or a metaphor for attention. An index becomes useful when it teaches the reader not to mistake shared vocabulary for shared reality.

One word, several kinds of claim

Polysemy is not confusion. Confusion begins when the senses are allowed to borrow evidence from one another.

Take Seed. In botany, a true seed contains an embryo and arises through plant reproduction. In agriculture, “seed potato” usually means vegetative planting material—a tuber or tuber piece. In scripture, seed can become ancestry, teaching, kingdom, promise or life that multiplies. In Potatoism, Seed is compressed future possibility. The word creates a bridge, but each side keeps its own evidence.

Or take Father. A biographical father is a historical person in a family relation. “Father” in Christian theology belongs to a specific doctrinal history. Father in Potatoverse canon is a project-defined source-facing role. The emotional power of the family word can illuminate all three, but it cannot make their claims interchangeable.

This is a general reading skill. Dictionaries list senses because meaning depends on use. Science often narrows ordinary words into technical definitions; religion can thicken words through ritual and narrative; subcultures can turn a word into a role or password. Before arguing about a word, ask what kind of object the speaker is trying to name.

Index test: did the word help you find the thing, or did the word quietly replace the thing?

What kind of thing did you search for?

A–Z mixes several kinds of entries on purpose. A person or recurring handle should open a dossier or biography; a project concept should open its strongest reader; a historical/religious term should open a tradition reader; an evidence system should open Sources; and an overloaded word should branch before the site decides what you meant.

Use A–Z as a doorway, not a definition engine

A name may carry aliases, titles and gifts. Those facets are retained in the entity ledger with provenance and strength, while A–Z shows only enough to identify the right record.

The authoritative meaning of a term still belongs to its canonical Room or knowledge owner. A–Z deliberately does not flatten a person into an archetype or a symbol into one meaning. “Dog” may refer to a literal animal, a biblical comparison, a project boundary role or a hostile historical label. “Tree” can be botanical, scriptural, architectural or the name of a social space. “Matthew” can be a person, a biblical name or a created Potato character. The index exists to expose those branches, not conceal them.

For deep character work, enter Beings & Cast and the active CIA Character Archive. For canonical project vocabulary, enter Core Identities & Roles. For dated events, use Timeline. For source authority and recovery state, use Archive & Sources. For terms that remain genuinely unresolved, use Questions rather than forcing them into a final definition.

Growing index

The build system may later derive a much larger machine-generated A–Z from canonical records, but this authored reader provides the stable human orientation layer. New names should enter only when the project can state what kind of object they are, which Room owns them, and whether their current meaning is documentary, literary, theological, comparative, scientific, formal or unresolved.
