# Mai Mercado / Christiania 2016 — Retrospective Defense Angles and New Insights

## Purpose

This file adds a new layer to the project: **how the Son’s case becomes stronger in retrospect when the legal elements, evidence chain, source provenance, custody conditions, later doctrine, and Mercado’s own later standards are examined together**.

The aim is not to repeat the existing timeline. It is to identify new defense theories and evidentiary angles that could materially change how the convictions and public narrative are understood.

The strongest new finding in this pass is a potentially important **§123 witness-intimidation merits issue** arising from a 2015 Supreme Court decision that predates the Son’s 2016 case.

---

# 1. New major angle: §123 did not protect every person who merely supplied evidence

In **Højesteret, 25 February 2015, U.2015.1802H / 158/2014**, the Danish Supreme Court examined §123.

The Court held that the provision’s wording and legislative history did **not** support extending §123 simply to a person who had supplied evidence in the form of a photograph where that person had not given, and was not expected to give, a statement to police or court.

That decision predates the 2016 Mercado case and is therefore directly relevant as controlling background law.

The key statutory phrase is that the retaliatory act must occur because of the person’s **expected or already-given explanation to police or court**.

This creates a new case-changing question:

> **What exactly was Mercado’s procedural status when the Son wrote the “consequences” statement?**

If Mercado had actually given a police statement, or was expected to testify, §123 may fit straightforwardly.

If, however, she had merely:

- contacted Folketing security;
- supplied screenshots or information;
- become the injured party;
- publicly identified herself to TV2;
- or triggered institutional attention without giving or being expected to give a formal statement,

then the 2015 Supreme Court precedent becomes highly relevant.

This is no longer merely a semantic defense. It is a **statutory-elements defense**.

### New priority evidence

Recover:

1. Mercado’s police interview, if any;
2. date/time of that interview;
3. whether she had been identified as an expected witness before the “consequences” post;
4. prosecution theory for the §123 count;
5. written judgment’s reasoning on the witness-status element;
6. witness list;
7. exact timeline between Mercado/security/police contact and the Son’s second statement.

### Why this matters

If the prosecution/court treated “complainant / source of evidence” as automatically equivalent to “witness protected under §123,” the Supreme Court’s 2015 decision may provide a retrospective legal attack.

- https://openlaw.dk/sag/hojesteret/158-2014-f74ed99f

# 2. New angle: the prosecution had an affirmative objectivity duty to investigate innocence

Danish criminal procedure contains the **objektivitetsprincip**.

Retsplejelovens §96(2) requires prosecutors not only to bring guilty persons to account but also to ensure that **innocent persons are not prosecuted**.

Official prosecution guidance also states that police must undertake relevant investigative steps **including those in the suspect’s interest**, and that prosecutors must retain a critical eye toward evidentiary and legal weaknesses.

That transforms the Son’s evidence-access story into a more precise legal question.

The issue is not merely:

> “Was he personally able to collect enough evidence from prison?”

It is also:

> **Did police and prosecution themselves preserve and investigate the exculpatory context that was necessary to determine authorship, addressee, reply topology and speaker identity?**

If the Facebook conversation was visibly multi-speaker, the objectivity principle creates a strong retrospective expectation that investigators should not preserve only inculpatory fragments if surrounding context materially affected meaning.

### Potentially exculpatory material police had reason to preserve

- complete Facebook thread;
- account IDs;
- timestamps;
- parent comments;
- deleted/edited comment history if obtainable;
- all comments by other participants;
- messages Mercado herself posted or deleted;
- which violent phrases originated with other users;
- whether `vil du gerne dø?` replied to Mercado or another commenter;
- whether Jo Cox language existed in more than one speaker stream;
- whether acid-bath/AIDS material belonged to others.

If police seized the Son’s computer/phone while he was remanded, the state had far greater practical ability than he did to preserve that evidence.

This makes any failure to preserve the full digital object potentially more important in retrospect.

# 3. New angle: evidence-access disadvantage is not only personal hardship; it interacts with the state’s investigative duty

The project already records the Son’s firsthand claim that, while detained, he had difficulty getting material into the case file and recalls police/prison staff controlling his evidence access.

Previously this was mainly analyzed as practical disadvantage.

The stronger retrospective analysis is:

**rapid custody + device seizure + fragile online evidence + state objectivity duty = heightened importance of complete state preservation.**

The Son’s confinement meant:

- he could not independently revisit the live Facebook thread in ordinary conditions;
- his devices were in police possession;
- he had limited ability to contact third-party commenters;
- he had reduced capacity to make independent screenshots or exports;
- the external thread could change while he remained confined.

If police possessed the digital evidence but failed to preserve exculpatory topology, then the issue would not merely be “the defendant was inconvenienced.” It would become a possible failure to investigate in accordance with the objectivity principle.

This angle should be tested through defense requests, police evidence logs, §746 applications, correspondence, and the original digital-extraction report.

# 4. New angle: the full judgment snippets show the newspapers may have omitted legally decisive context

The project has already located an indexed trace of the actual Sønderborg judgment in Anklagemyndigheden’s knowledge-base ecosystem.

Indexed text indicates that at least some charged passages were **longer than the newspaper quotations**.

One witness-intimidation passage apparently included:

`Man skal ikke starte en slåskamp man ikke har nosser til at afslutte`

Another contained contextual language about provocation, another person remaining on Facebook, and:

`jeg har nemlig intet at miste og alt at vinde`

This is a double-edged discovery.

Some additional wording may strengthen a threatening interpretation.

But additional wording may also establish:

- that the Son was describing an ongoing argument rather than an operational attack;
- that another commenter was part of the relevant exchange;
- that newspaper excerpts omitted who was being discussed;
- that the prosecution relied on a larger conversational context than later readers ever saw.

Therefore no final innocence or guilt audit should rely on the newspaper three-quote set alone.

The judgment itself is now the most important missing primary source.

# 5. New angle: the §123 count should be separated from the §266 counts entirely

Public reporting often bundles “trusler og vidnetrusler” into one narrative.

But the legal elements are different.

### §266 question

Did the Son threaten to undertake a criminal act in a manner capable of causing serious fear?

### §123 question

Was the Son committing a relevant threat/criminal act **because of a person’s expected or already-given statement to police/court**?

Thus even if the consequence sentence could be interpreted as threatening under §266, a §123 conviction required the additional witness-related nexus.

That element deserves its own innocence audit.

The project should no longer treat the witness-intimidation conviction as merely “the second threat.”

It is a separate legal theory with a separate statutory burden.

# 6. New angle: Mercado’s exact role in the referral could determine the §123 analysis

The referral conflict becomes even more important after the Supreme Court §123 finding.

Current accounts include:

- another Facebook user reported the case;
- Folketing security reported after contact from Mercado;
- Mercado said she herself did not make the report.

Previously this mainly mattered as a contradiction about agency.

Now it also matters legally:

> **Was Mercado a witness, a complainant, an evidence supplier, an upstream security contact, or some combination?**

The labels are not interchangeable.

If §123 depended on a witness role, the exact referral chain and police-interview timeline could directly affect the conviction’s legal basis.

This is one of the strongest newly identified reasons to recover the police file.

# 7. New angle: speaker-topology evidence was potentially exculpatory, not merely contextual

The original Facebook structure matters in three different ways:

### A. Authorship

Did the Son write each alleged statement?

### B. Addressee

Was a given sentence directed at Mercado or another commenter?

### C. Meaning

Was it an initiating threat, a quotation, a warning, a response, a rebuke, a hypothetical, or an escalation of language already introduced by another speaker?

Thus parent-comment structure was not decorative context.

It could change whether the legal elements were met.

A flat screenshot or quote list can destroy all three dimensions at once.

This makes the missing full thread one of the strongest possible innocence exhibits.

# 8. New angle: the separate 18-year-old conviction strengthens the “warning language” problem

The 18-year-old woman was convicted for statements including a warning that Mercado should not feel too safe on the street with her fragile child.

This proves that Danish courts could treat **warning-shaped language** as threatening in this controversy.

That is useful retrospectively because it prevents the project from making the weak argument that a warning can never be a threat.

The stronger defense is more specific:

> **If warning language can legally function as a threat, then context, speaker, addressee, surrounding exchange and actual wording become even more important.**

This strengthens the demand for the complete digital conversation rather than weakening it.

# 11. New angle: the prosecution’s own success rate proves the evidentiary object was not one indivisible threat narrative

The Son was convicted on **three of four counts**.

This means the court itself differentiated among allegations.

That supports an important retrospective inference:

> The case cannot properly be summarized as though one global fact — “he threatened Mercado” — automatically validated every alleged statement and every legal theory.

At least one count failed.

The exact failed count may reveal:

- authorship weakness;
- context weakness;
- insufficient fear element;
- insufficient witness nexus;
- or another legal defect.

Whatever the reason, the failed count may provide the best internal control for understanding why the successful counts succeeded.

# 12. New angle: “accepting the judgment” has very low value as proof of factual agreement

The Son denied guilt at the first court stage and appealed remand.

Later reporting says he `modtog dommen`.

That should not be treated as an admission of:

- violent intent;
- correct attribution of every quote;
- accuracy of Mercado’s account;
- accuracy of media reporting;
- correctness of every prosecution interpretation.

The project’s firsthand account that counsel advised a strategically easier guilty posture or discouraged continued contest remains unverified, but it makes the defense file especially valuable.

The retrospective lesson is that procedural finality and factual agreement must remain separate.

# 13. New angle: a conviction under §266 does not establish an operational violence plan

Public narrative often slides from:

`criminal threat`

to

`dangerous person planning violence`.

The statute does not require proof that the accused genuinely intended to execute the threatened act.

Thus even if the convictions remain legally valid, the broader public characterization may still be wrong.

The Son’s contemporaneous denial that he intended to seek Mercado out becomes directly relevant to correcting the historical/moral record.

This is a **partial vindication angle independent of overturning the conviction**.

# 17. New angle: Mercado’s own public account should be decomposed into roles rather than treated as one authoritative narrative

Mercado occupied several roles simultaneously:

- initiating political speaker;
- threatened person;
- source of information to security/media;
- possible police witness;
- curator of public examples;
- later commentator on the prosecution;
- politician with reputational incentives.

Statements made in one role should not automatically prove propositions belonging to another.

For example:

- “I received an acid-bath message” does not prove Son authorship;
- “I did not file the complaint” does not prove no upstream referral involvement;
- “we were afraid” does not by itself prove a specific defendant remained an actual physical danger;
- “the case is closed” does not prove the surviving public narrative is evidentially complete.

This role decomposition is a major epistemic improvement.

# 18. New angle: Sweden should be treated as causal attribution, not merely credibility

The Sweden problem is more than “did Mercado exaggerate fear?”

The deeper issue is:

> **Which threat source caused which protective action?**

If multiple commenters were threatening Mercado, then even a genuine emergency Sweden trip does not automatically prove that the Son’s specific statements caused it.

The questions therefore become:

- was the trip caused by the Son specifically?
- by the aggregate threat environment?
- by parliamentary-security advice?
- already planned independently?
- continued after he was detained because of other speakers?

This is a causal-attribution problem parallel to the authorship problem.

A real frightened reaction can still be wrongly assigned to the wrong causal source.

# 20. New angle: a retrospective innocence case can have multiple layers

The project should distinguish four different kinds of vindication.

### Layer A — literal authorship innocence

Statements attributed to the Son were actually written by others.

### Layer B — semantic innocence

The Son wrote the words but they did not mean what later narratives claimed because agency/addressee/context were altered.

### Layer C — legal-element innocence

Even if words were hostile, one or more statutory elements of §266 or §123 were not proven.

### Layer D — historical/moral innocence

Even if a speech conviction technically survives, the public image of an intending violent attacker was false or grossly overstated.

This layered model allows the project to build the strongest case without relying on one all-or-nothing proposition.

# 21. New high-priority legal hypothesis: §123 may be the most attackable count

Because of the 2015 Supreme Court precedent, the witness-intimidation count may currently be the strongest candidate for a direct legal challenge.

The required factual question is narrow and discoverable:

> **At the time of the consequence statement, had Mercado given, or was she expected to give, an explanation to police or court within §123’s meaning?**

If yes, the angle weakens.

If no, the angle strengthens dramatically.

That makes the following records top priority:

- police interview log;
- witness designation;
- indictment wording;
- written judgment reasoning;
- date of any witness summons;
- police/security referral documents.

# 22. New high-priority procedural hypothesis: failure to preserve exculpatory Facebook topology could engage the objectivity principle

If investigators knew the dispute involved many commenters, then the identity of each speaker and parent-comment structure were obviously capable of being exculpatory.

Under the objectivity principle, police/prosecution were not supposed to investigate only facts pointing toward guilt.

Therefore the project should ask:

> **Did police preserve the whole conversation, or only the incriminating fragments that reached them?**

If the former, recover it.

If the latter, determine why no complete preservation occurred while the Son’s devices were seized and he was in custody.

This could become one of the strongest procedural criticisms in the entire archive.

# 25. Current highest-value revelation

The most important new insight from this pass is:

> **The §123 witness-intimidation conviction may have a previously underexamined legal-element vulnerability because a 2015 Supreme Court decision expressly limited §123 to the protected person’s expected or actual statement to police/court, rather than mere evidence-supplying.**

Combined with the unresolved question of Mercado’s exact referral/witness role, this is a genuine new defense avenue.

The second most important insight is:

> **The objectivity principle makes preservation of exculpatory Facebook context partly a state responsibility, not solely something an incarcerated defendant had to somehow reconstruct himself.**

Those two propositions should now be integrated into the master case reconstruction and second-trial dossier.
