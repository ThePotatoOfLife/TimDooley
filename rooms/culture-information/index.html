Culture & Information

Culture is what people teach one another without always calling it a lesson: what counts as funny, rude, sacred, embarrassing, beautiful, obvious, suspicious, cool, shameful, worth remembering—or not worth noticing at all.

You are already inside it

Culture begins where explanation becomes unnecessary

A room has become a culture when somebody new can enter it and feel that everybody else already knows how to move.

Think of all the things nobody had to formally legislate for you today: how close to stand to a stranger, when a joke has gone too far, what clothes look serious, which accent sounds trustworthy, whether silence is awkward, whether a potato is merely food, whether a blue check means prestige, whether a screenshot counts as proof. Culture lives in those learned expectations. It is not a decorative layer placed on top of society. It is part of the machinery by which a social world becomes legible.

Anthropology widened the word far beyond museums and “high culture.” Culture can include socially shared patterns of thought, feeling and behavior; symbols and meanings; material objects; institutions; habits; stories; rules; categories; jokes; rituals; bodies; spaces and technologies. It is learned, transmitted, argued over, copied badly, repaired, commercialized and transformed. A culture is therefore never only a list of beliefs. It is also a way of noticing, doing and interpreting.

That is the purpose of this room. Not to tell you that “memes spread” or that “groups have norms,” but to give you enough lenses that you can walk into a church, a football terrace, a punk show, a Discord server, a courtroom, a family dinner, a workplace, a fandom—or the Potato House itself—and begin to see the invisible architecture holding the scene together.

Not one definition — several useful lenses

Culture is meaning, practice, inheritance and a toolkit for action

No single sentence captures everything scholars mean by culture, and that is useful. Different definitions illuminate different mechanisms. Clifford Geertz famously pushed anthropology toward interpretation: a wink is physically similar to an eye twitch, but culturally it can be flirtation, conspiracy, parody or mockery. The movement alone is not enough; the shared meaning makes it social. Ann Swidler later offered another powerful lens: culture supplies repertoires—habits, skills, styles and symbols—from which people construct strategies for living.

Meaning

Culture gives signs a second life. A color becomes mourning, a flag becomes a nation, a ring becomes a promise, a frog becomes a meme, a potato becomes a character. The physical object matters, but the shared interpretation is what makes the object culturally dense.

Practice

People learn culture by doing it: greeting, cooking, dressing, arguing, praying, queuing, gaming, moderating, flirting, posting, apologizing. Many rules are embodied before they can be explained.

Inheritance

Culture allows strangers born at different times to inherit a partly shared world. Recipes, idioms, calendars, songs, rituals, law, interface conventions and family stories carry solutions—and sometimes old mistakes—forward.

Toolkit

A culture does not turn everyone into the same person. It offers a repertoire. Different people select different pieces, combine them differently, resist some, exaggerate others and invent new uses for old forms.

Contest

Culture is never perfectly shared. People fight over what a symbol means, who owns a tradition, what counts as authentic, which words are acceptable, whose memory becomes official and whether a rule still deserves obedience.

A caution that improves the whole room: cultures are not sealed boxes and people are not walking specimens of a culture. Contemporary anthropology is wary of treating groups as internally uniform, timeless or radically separate. Culture is patterned sharing, not total sameness.

Field method

You can learn a culture by watching what happens before, during and after somebody breaks an unwritten rule

Rules are easiest to see when they fail. Walk into an unfamiliar scene and you may not know the etiquette. Laugh too loudly, use the wrong word, sit in the wrong chair, praise the wrong band, ask the forbidden question—or simply fail to understand a reference—and suddenly the invisible map appears through correction. Somebody looks at you. Somebody explains. Somebody mocks. Somebody rescues you. That response tells you what the group is protecting.

What is sacred here?Not necessarily religious. Which object, person, value, story or memory cannot be treated casually without consequence?
What is ridiculous here?Humor marks boundaries with extraordinary efficiency. Who may be mocked, what may be parodied, and which jokes require insider knowledge?
What buys status?Money? age? suffering? technical skill? authenticity? obscure knowledge? seniority? follower count? generosity? proximity to a founder?
What is expensive to admit?Ignorance, doubt, boredom, fear, changing your mind, liking something mainstream, not understanding the lore—these costs reveal the moral economy of the group.
What does a newcomer copy first?Vocabulary, dress, posture, usernames, citations, catchphrases, enemies, punctuation, emotes, ritual times. Copying reveals the shortest route into belonging.
Can a person become new?Healthy memory records the past without making an old role permanent. Watch whether correction, forgiveness, reinvention and exit are culturally possible.

Culture is often visible in the correction. The official values tell you what a group says it is. The handling of embarrassment, dissent, mistakes, outsiders and status tells you what the group repeatedly becomes.

Subculture · scene · taste-public · neo-tribe

A subculture is not merely a smaller culture. It is a place where difference becomes legible to itself.

The simple textbook definition is “a culture within a larger culture.” Useful, but too flat. Classic subculture studies asked how groups—especially youth scenes—used music, fashion, slang and style to make identities that did not fit comfortably inside dominant expectations. Dick Hebdige treated style as meaningful: ordinary objects could be appropriated and recombined until clothing, hair, music or posture became a compressed social argument.

But not every modern group is a stable tribe. Later sociologists challenged rigid subculture models. People move through scenes, fandoms and lifestyles; memberships overlap; identities can be temporary; commercial products can carry the look of a scene far beyond its original community. “Subculture” remains useful, but sometimes scene, taste culture, affinity space or neo-tribe better describes a loose network whose members gather around a mood, practice or aesthetic and then disperse again.

Style

A safety pin can become an argument

Subcultural style works because meaning is movable. A mass-produced object can be worn “wrong,” placed in a new context and made to signal refusal, irony, belonging or aggression. Once the mainstream recognizes the signal, however, it can imitate, market and neutralize it.

Status

Knowing the right obscure thing can become currency

Sarah Thornton’s idea of subcultural capital captures a familiar phenomenon: prestige inside a scene may come from knowing the earlier band, the forgotten lore, the correct venue, the uncool commercial imitation, the right way to pronounce a name—or knowing what not to explain to outsiders.

This produces a delicious paradox. A scene can be organized around escaping the mainstream while constantly measuring itself against the mainstream. The moment outsiders understand the haircut, the phrase, the meme or the sound, insiders may move again. Difference has to be reproduced.

Ritual, taboo and belonging

Some cultural rules become powerful because people perform them with their bodies

Ritual is often mistaken for empty repetition. In cultural analysis, repetition is exactly what makes ritual interesting. Birthdays, funerals, chants, weddings, introductions, livestream greetings, Friday prayers, handshakes, graduation robes, match-day songs and “good morning” routines create patterned time. Victor Turner’s work on rites of passage emphasized liminality: the threshold state in which an old status has been left but the new one has not fully arrived. Those thresholds can intensify solidarity because ordinary hierarchy is temporarily rearranged.

Mary Douglas showed another way to read a culture: look at what it calls dirty, polluted, impure or “out of place.” Taboos protect categories. A thing becomes disturbing not only because it is dangerous but because it crosses a boundary the culture depends on. The same logic appears in secular spaces: inappropriate clothes at a funeral, spoilers in a fandom, speaking business language at a wake, using a sacred meme incorrectly, or bringing a private screenshot into a public arena.

Bodies, rooms, food, tools

Culture is not trapped inside beliefs. It is built into things.

A dining table teaches a geometry of family. A courtroom teaches hierarchy through elevation and distance. A nightclub organizes bodies through sound, darkness and a door policy. A smartphone creates a permanent pocket-stage from which a person can perform to absent audiences. A uniform makes a role visible before the wearer speaks. A recipe stores history in procedure.

This is why material culture matters. Objects are not passive illustrations of ideas; they can make certain actions easier, harder or more thinkable. The shape of a room affects who can gather. A platform button affects which response is cheap. An archive schema affects which parts of a life are easy to retrieve. A potato can be food, agricultural history, famine memory, peasant symbol, toy, joke, emoji, avatar or theology depending on the cultural system that receives it.

If you want to understand a culture, do not only ask what people believe. Ask what their world is made of, where their bodies go, what their hands repeatedly touch, and which objects have acquired more meaning than their material should be able to carry.

Folklore did not disappear when Wi-Fi arrived

A meme is closer to a folk song than to a billboard

Digital culture did not invent copying, remix or communal authorship. Jokes, legends, recipes, songs, rumors and proverbs have always moved by being retold. What digital networks changed is the speed, scale, visibility and retrievability of variation. Limor Shifman’s work on internet memes emphasizes that memes matter culturally because people do not only receive them—they recreate them. Henry Jenkins’s work on participatory culture similarly foregrounds communities that produce, remix, circulate and collaboratively interpret media.

That makes the life of a meme more interesting than “viral or not viral.” A cultural object can mutate while keeping a recognizable skeleton. One person changes the caption, another the image, another the target, another the moral meaning. The object survives by being changed.

One joke, observed properly

Event: somebody says a funny line in a chat.

Folklore: other people repeat it with small variations. The exact wording matters less than knowing the form.

Password: understanding the joke signals that you were there—or at least that you know the lore.

Status: somebody performs the joke especially well and becomes associated with it.

Archive: a screenshot outlives the conversation and becomes the easiest version to retrieve.

Mutation: newcomers use the phrase in circumstances the original speakers never imagined.

Question: is the living culture still playing with the joke, or has the archive turned one old joke into somebody’s permanent identity?

When the room has an algorithm

Platforms do not merely host culture; their affordances enter the culture

On a village square, visibility is limited by bodies, distance and time. On a platform, visibility is also organized by ranking, recommendation, moderation, monetization, search, notification design, follower graphs and interface defaults. Those systems do not fully determine what people do, but they change the cost of doing it.

A “like” makes one kind of approval frictionless. A repost turns transmission into a button. A public follower count turns attention into a visible status metric. Ephemeral stories create a different memory culture from permanent searchable posts. Character limits reward compression; quote-posting makes commentary travel with the object; recommendation systems can make a tiny scene feel omnipresent to the people repeatedly shown it.

Internet culture therefore has at least two authors: the people making meanings and the technical environment shaping which meanings are easy to repeat. That is why the adjacent Platform and Information Ecology rooms exist. Here, the important insight is that interface design eventually becomes etiquette, expectation, status and habit—and therefore becomes cultural.

Taste is never only taste

Culture distributes dignity, prestige and the power to classify

Pierre Bourdieu’s work on taste made a lasting point: preferences do not float free of social position. What counts as refined, vulgar, educated, authentic or embarrassing can reproduce distinctions between groups. Even when people sincerely love what they love, judgments of taste can still become judgments of people.

Subcultures invent their own versions of the same game. Mainstream prestige may be inverted: obscurity becomes valuable, commercial success becomes suspicious, technical knowledge becomes cool, “I knew it before it was popular” becomes a claim to rank. The currencies change; the status machinery remains recognizable.

This also means classification is power. Calling something art, spam, religion, cringe, scholarship, propaganda, a cult, a fandom, tradition or “just a joke” changes how audiences approach it. Those labels should not be treated as neutral containers. They can open doors, close them, reduce complexity or grant legitimacy.

Especially here: “cult,” “high-control,” “extremist,” “criminal” and similar labels need explicit criteria and evidence. Strange aesthetics, intense enthusiasm, outsider vocabulary or a shared audience do not establish coercion, command or guilt.

The mainstream is not the opposite shore

Subculture and mainstream continuously manufacture each other

A subculture often defines itself against something: ordinary taste, commercial media, parents, institutions, respectability, political consensus, another scene. But the larger culture is watching too. Fashion photographs the street. Advertisers borrow underground aesthetics. Record labels market rebellion. Platforms package “authenticity.” Yesterday’s insult becomes a brand identity; yesterday’s secret music appears in a supermarket.

Classic subculture theory sometimes over-romanticized resistance, as if every unusual style were automatically political. Later research makes the picture messier and more believable. People join scenes for pleasure, friendship, aesthetics, experimentation, romance, escape, boredom, skill, identity, resistance—or several at once. A culture does not have to be revolutionary to be meaningful.

The useful question is not “did the mainstream steal it?” in every case. Ask what changed during translation. Was the object detached from the community that gave it meaning? Did commercialization widen participation, flatten the symbolism, fund the creators, erase the origin, or produce a new culture altogether? Often several of those things happen simultaneously.

A local specimen

The Potato House is itself a culture-making machine, so this room should study its own floorboards

Take the potato. Biologically and agriculturally, it is a tuber. Historically, it carries stories of cultivation, subsistence, empire, famine, migration, class and survival. In popular culture it can be humble, comic, ugly, comforting or absurd. In this project it gathers additional authored meanings: Potato of Life, gardener, root, food, witness, character, cosmology and joke.

Then take 🍟. Outside this project it ordinarily means fries or fast food. Inside the Potatoverse it has accumulated extra uses through repetition and interaction: recognition, “fried,” judgment, a comic bonk, an observing symbol, and at times a sword. That is culture happening in miniature. A sign acquires a local surplus of meaning because people repeatedly use it inside a shared history.

The important part is not to freeze that symbol in a dictionary. Living symbols remain negotiable. Somebody can misuse them, parody them, reinterpret them, reject them, teach them badly, or invent a use that later becomes canonical. A dead glossary tells you what a sign once meant. A culture tells you what people can still do with it.

A culture worth living in

Healthy culture is not culture without boundaries. It is culture whose boundaries can still learn.

Every durable culture selects. It remembers some things more than others. It has heroes, embarrassments, good taste, bad taste, sacred stories, stupid jokes and things that simply do not belong. Total openness would not abolish culture; it would abolish enough continuity for anybody to recognize one.

The more useful distinction is between living boundary and frozen boundary. A living culture can explain itself to newcomers without requiring permanent ignorance from outsiders. It can preserve standards without making dissent a metaphysical crime. It can remember injury without requiring somebody to remain the same person forever. It can permit parody because its identity is stronger than one joke. It can encounter another culture without either instantly surrendering or needing to destroy it.

This is also where “culture versus cultic narrowing” becomes useful if handled carefully. The danger is not weirdness, intensity or uncommon belief. The danger is a social system becoming increasingly unable to process disconfirming information: loyalty replacing evidence, purity replacing curiosity, departure becoming betrayal, one authority becoming unrevisable, outsiders becoming necessary enemies, and contradiction becoming proof of corruption rather than an invitation to inspect the claim.

A culture is alive when the next generation can inherit it without being sentenced to repeat it exactly.

A five-minute cultural fieldwalk

Try this anywhere people gather

You do not need an expedition to practice cultural analysis. Sit in a café, watch a livestream, enter a game lobby, read a subreddit, attend a church service, visit a workplace or observe your own family meal. For five minutes, stop asking whether you agree with the people. Ask how the world is being made.

  • Minute 1Inventory the repeated objects, words, gestures, sounds and visual symbols. Do not interpret yet.
  • Minute 2Watch status. Who gets interrupted? Who can break the rules? Whose approval changes the room?
  • Minute 3Watch teaching. How does a newcomer learn—explanation, imitation, ridicule, correction, ritual, interface cues?
  • Minute 4Watch boundaries. What would make somebody look like an outsider even before they explicitly disagreed?
  • Minute 5Watch change. Is anybody allowed to introduce a new practice, repair a norm, retire a joke or reinterpret a symbol?

Then ask the uncomfortable version: which of those mechanisms are operating on you while you are observing everybody else?
