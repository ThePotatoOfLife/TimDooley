Systems & Dynamics

This Room studies what changes, what constrains change and what information is lost when a complicated reality is compressed into states, graphs, flows, feedback loops and control models.

Systems analogy must identify its variables, scope and information loss. A graph hub is not automatically a controller; observation is not actuation; a symbolic center is not evidence of causal ownership.

The language of changing systems

Six words for seeing motion instead of snapshots

A system is not fully described by its parts. You also need to know what can be seen, what can be changed, what remembers the past, where delays live and which disturbances the system can absorb.

What condition is it in?State

The minimal description needed to predict relevant future behavior under the model.

What returns to influence itself?Feedback

A consequence re-enters the system as an input. Reinforcing feedback amplifies; balancing feedback resists change.

What can be inferred from outputs?Observability

Whether hidden internal state can be reconstructed from what the system lets you measure.

What can inputs actually move?Controllability

Whether available interventions can drive the system toward the states you care about.

How does history remain?Hysteresis

The path into a state affects the path out. Restoring the old input may not restore the old condition.

Where does time hide?Lag

Delay between cause, response and observation. Lags can make good control look weak and bad control look stable until too late.

Carry this out of the Room: before saying a system is “stuck” or “controlled,” ask about state, feedback, observability, controllability, hysteresis and lag.

Systems language is useful because it separates roles that ordinary prose often fuses. A node can be visible without controlling anything. An anchor can constrain geometry without owning the system. A low-degree gate can dominate a route while a famous hub remains bypassable. These distinctions matter in biology, infrastructure, platforms and project symbolism alike.

State and transition come before narrative.

Define the state, admissible inputs and transition rule before describing a trajectory as ascent, decline, repair or capture. If the state variables are not explicit, the model may be a taxonomy rather than a dynamical system.

Observation is not control.

In control theory, measurements and actuating inputs are different channels. Seeing, predicting, naming or attracting attention does not prove the ability to move the state. The project uses this distinction to keep Eye/observer language separate from Leash/controller language.

Centrality is not dominance.

Graph degree, eigenvector or betweenness centrality measure forms of prominence. A dominator or cut answers a different question: which node or set is unavoidable on a particular route? A small gateway can therefore be structurally decisive without being globally central.

The right systems question is rarely “who is at the center?” It is “what intervention changes which reachable states, at what cost?”

Agency has several coordinates.

Reachable alternatives, robustness to disturbance, intervention cost, reversibility and exit authority can all vary independently. “Can reach the target” is weaker than “can reach it reliably and affordably.”

Real systems usually have several controllers.

Institutions, people, environments, feedback and disturbances can pull a trajectory simultaneously. Modeling one universal owner is often less informative than identifying the competing inputs, constraints and objectives that shape the path.

Three systems that behave differently from intuition

Lake Veluwe · hysteresis and alternative stable states.

Scheffer and colleagues used shallow-lake ecology to show that a system can flip abruptly between clear-water vegetation and turbid phytoplankton states. In Lake Veluwe, the phosphorus level at which vegetation collapsed was not the same as the level required for recovery. History mattered: reducing the input back to the old value did not instantly restore the old state. That is hysteresis.

Source: Scheffer et al., Nature 413 (2001) ↗

Silicon Valley Bank · reinforcing feedback in hours.

SVB relied on a deposit base that was more than 90% uninsured. On 9 March 2023, $42B in deposits left and another $100B was staged for withdrawal the next day. Loss realization weakened confidence; withdrawals forced liquidity pressure; visible stress intensified withdrawal incentives. The useful systems object is the feedback loop, not a morality tale about one node.

Source: FDIC, lessons from 2023 regional bank failures ↗

Power grids · cascades depend on topology and controls.

NIST modelling shows how failures of critical grid components can propagate into cascading outages and how the location/capacity of storage or protection changes the outcome. A cascade is therefore not “one failure becoming many” by magic: load redistributes, limits are crossed, protective actions change flows, and further components can fail.

Source: NIST cascading-failure study ↗

Four more dynamics: queues, contagion, control and resilience

Queueing · Little's Law makes delay visible.

For a stable system, the long-run average number of items in the system obeys L = λW: average inventory or work-in-process equals throughput multiplied by average time in system. If a service completes 120 requests per second and the average request spends 0.25 seconds in the system, the average number in flight is about 30 requests. The equation does not tell us the full waiting-time distribution or why a bottleneck exists, but it immediately connects throughput, congestion and latency.

Source: Glynn & Whitt, Mathematics of Operations Research — L = λW ↗

Contagion · SIR turns spread into state transitions.

A basic susceptible–infectious–recovered model treats people as moving among three compartments. In one common continuous form, dS/dt = -βSI/N, dI/dt = βSI/N - γI, and dR/dt = γI. Contact intensity β and recovery rate γ shape whether the infectious compartment initially grows or shrinks. The useful lesson is structural: contagion is not simply “one node influences another”; it depends on the susceptible pool, contact process and exit/recovery dynamics.

Source: CDC Center for Forecasting and Outbreak Analytics — transmission models ↗

TCP congestion control · a controller can be distributed and feedback-driven.

TCP senders maintain a congestion window (cwnd) that limits outstanding data. During congestion avoidance, the standard increases the window gradually—roughly one full-sized segment per round-trip time—while detected loss causes the sender to reduce its sending state. The network does not need one central traffic controller for this feedback loop to regulate load: local senders observe acknowledgments/loss and alter their own admissible rate.

Source: RFC 5681 — TCP Congestion Control ↗

Cyber resilience · redundancy changes the failure surface, but shared failure modes matter.

NIST treats resilience as maintaining required capability under adversity and recovering within mission-relevant time. Its systems guidance treats redundancy as surplus capacity or replication, but warns that homogeneous redundant resources can share the same failure mechanism; diversity and protection of redundant resources therefore matter. A second copy is not automatically a second independent path.

Sources: NIST resilience glossary ↗ · NIST SP 800-160 Vol. 2 — cyber-resilient systems ↗

Minimal variable ledger

Queue: λ = arrival/throughput rate, W = average time in system, L = average number present. Contagion: S/I/R = compartment populations, β = effective contact/transmission rate, γ = recovery/removal rate. Control: cwnd = sender congestion state, ACK/loss = feedback signals. Resilience: required capability, disturbance, degraded state, redundancy/diversity, and recovery time must be named rather than replaced by the word “resilient.”

Translate the cases into project operators carefully

Swamp can borrow from hysteresis only at the structural level: a state may persist after the original forcing weakens. Drain can borrow from reinforcing feedback: repeated movement narrows reachable alternatives. Door can represent a threshold only if the state really changes across it. Forge can represent intervention only if the intervention changes the governing feedback, not merely the story told about it.

A system has changed when its reachable states, feedbacks or constraints change—not when the label on the same trajectory changes.

Compression audit

Whenever a system is represented as a graph or state model, ask what was discarded: spatial geometry, timing, uncertainty, heterogeneity, hidden variables, agency, semantics or causal direction. The model is strongest when its omissions are explicit.
