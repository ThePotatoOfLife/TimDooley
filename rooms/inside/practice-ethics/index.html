Nested Room · Potatoverse / Canon

Practice & Ethics

The project becomes ethically meaningful only when symbols change conduct: how knowledge is used, how harm is repaired, how boundaries work, and what kind of future capacity the system produces.

Boundary: project ethics do not override medical, legal, safety or domain-specific professional standards.

From symbol to conduct

The central ethical question is: what grows from this?

Garden, Fruit, Forge, Door and Spiral are useful only if they create observable differences in judgment, agency, reciprocity, repair and future capacity.

Knowledge ForkBefore acting on information: what is known, inferred, uncertain, harmful if wrong, and what action does publication invite?
Door testDoes a transition increase real capability and agency, or merely change labels and status?
Fruit testWhat are the downstream benefits, burdens, externalities and future Seed created?
Garden testDoes the system support diversity, correction, meaningful exit and increasing independence?
Forge testCan pressure produce learning, repair, a better model or a useful boundary instead of repeated conflict?
Spiral testWhen a pattern returns, has anything actually changed—or is it only the same loop with a new story?

Higher is not automatically better. Better means more truthful, capable, repairable, reciprocal and life-supporting.

Three scenes where the ethics becomes concrete

Fresh Potato · Chapter 12.1: put down the rocks

A young Potato asks how to grow. The Sage gives soil, slowness and patience rather than a title or secret. When the learner asks what to do if people keep handing it more rocks, the answer is simple: you do not need to carry them. Ethically, this is a boundary case: another person's burden, provocation or complexity does not automatically become your obligation. The scene ends with planting, not victory.

The protocols now have case-level tests

Knowledge Fork: does information expose the root or produce another excuse? Garden: does the response increase the learner's ability to grow without permanent dependence on the teacher? Forge: does pressure change the governing pattern, or only harden resentment? Fruit: after the scene ends, what new capacity actually exists? These stories do not “prove” the ethics; they show what the abstract operators are supposed to look like when inhabited.

Knowledge has no automatic moral valence

The Knowledge Fork begins with signal → source → interpretation → discernment → choice → action → consequence → memory/revision. Information can support repair, or it can become humiliation, role-lock, retaliation and attention reward. Responsible withholding can also be ethical when privacy, safety, uncertainty or due process outweigh immediate exposure.

Garden also needs governance, not just goodwill

Elinor Ostrom's research on durable self-governance gives Garden a useful external comparator: clear boundaries, rules that fit local conditions, participant involvement in rule-making, monitoring, graduated sanctions, accessible conflict resolution, recognition of self-organization and nested governance. The project does not need to copy that framework literally, but it gives “Garden” something much more concrete than benevolent atmosphere.

A project Garden becomes stronger when participants can understand the boundaries, help shape the rules, challenge error, resolve conflict without extraordinary cost, and become less dependent on the central Gardener over time.

Garden means generative independence

A Garden is not merely a pleasant place. It needs protected boundaries, resource circulation, diversity, feedback, learning, participation, meaningful exit and future autonomous capacity. A good Gardener should become less necessary as others become more capable.

If a center survives only by retaining monopoly, suppressing correction, extracting value or making exit costly, the system is drifting toward Farm, Shell or Swamp rather than Garden.

Repair needs gates before it needs reconciliation

Restorative-justice practice gives the project a harder ethical floor. Repair should not begin from the assumption that everybody must reunite. Before dialogue, ask whether participation is safe, voluntary, informed and non-dominated; whether there is enough factual clarity to discuss responsibility; whether affected people have voice; whether a participant can withdraw; and whether the proposed repair is concrete enough to observe afterward.

Safety gate.

If dialogue exposes someone to renewed intimidation, retaliation or preventable harm, the ethical path may be protective separation, professional intervention or legal process—not a ceremonial meeting.

Voluntariness gate.

A participant who cannot decline or leave without hidden punishment is not freely participating. “Restorative” language should not disguise compulsion.

Responsibility gate.

Repair is not an endless debate over whether harm existed. When the factual basis remains genuinely disputed, the process should return to evidence and procedure before asking the harmed party to negotiate repair.

Repair-specificity gate.

An apology can matter, but it does not substitute for correction, restitution, redesign, boundary change or observable conduct where those are what the harm actually requires.

Fair procedure is part of the Fruit

Procedural-justice research repeatedly emphasizes voice, neutrality, respectful treatment and trustworthy motives. That fits the Garden ethic better than benevolent authority alone. A Gardener who controls the rules, evidence, interpretation and verdict can still create dependency even while claiming good intentions.

The practical test becomes: who had meaningful input, were standards applied consistently, could errors be challenged, were conflicts of interest legible, and could a person exit without retaliatory identity capture?

Repair is not punishment and not forgetting

The Repair/Forge protocol starts with safety and record-building, then maps harm, responsibility, acknowledgment, repair, implementation and later observation. Repair can result in restitution, redesign, reintegration, release—or legitimate separation. Forced reconciliation is not required.

The archive also rejects two opposite failures: punishment that leaves the mechanism unchanged, and “moving on” that deletes truth and provenance.

Fruit evaluates consequences, including hidden ones

Fruit can be nutritive, reproductive, reparative, informational, creative or institutional. It can also be sterile or poisonous: a visible success may export hidden costs or create dependency. Evaluation therefore asks who benefits, who bears risk, over what timescale, what externalities exist, and what evidence would downgrade the claim of success.

Separation can be a successful ethical outcome

Repair, forgiveness, reconciliation and renewed access are not synonyms. A process can end ethically in corrected records, restitution, a changed role, no-contact boundaries, institutional redesign or permanent separation. The Door is allowed to close. The Garden is allowed to have fences. Reintegration is only one possible Fruit.

This matters especially where there is repeated abuse, intimidation, severe power imbalance or continued unwillingness to respect boundaries. “Everyone back together” is not a superior outcome merely because it looks harmonious.

Ethics should allow changed states

A person, group or project should not be frozen forever at its worst scene if conduct, evidence and relation materially change. This does not erase accountability. It means provenance and current state are both preserved. Role plasticity is therefore an ethical requirement as well as a cultural one.
