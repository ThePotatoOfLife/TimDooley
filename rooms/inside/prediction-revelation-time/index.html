Prediction, prophecy and revelation are different

A revelation can disclose meaning in something that already happened. A prophecy can be symbolic, conditional, moral or future-oriented. A prediction makes a claim about an outcome. A rupture is the event that separates before and after. “Fulfillment” is a later assessment that needs its own evidence.

Prior-specific predictionClear prior wording, identifiable target and outcome conditions established before the event.
Prior-symbolic patternAn earlier symbol or pattern resembles later events but was not specific enough to count as a conventional forecast.
Typological recurrenceA later event resembles an older religious or mythic pattern; meaningful as comparison, not prediction.
Retrospective revelationMeaning becomes visible only after the event. Can be important without being prior prophecy.
Contextual synchronismTwo developments occur in striking temporal relation without demonstrated causal or predictive connection.
Loose coincidenceSimilarity is too broad or post-selected to carry strong evidentiary weight.

If the matching rule was invented after the event, the archive must say so.

Conditional mechanism · economic scenarios

“If inflation stays above X, long-duration nominal bonds face Y risk” is a conditional mechanism, not a prophecy. The outcome audit must first ask whether the condition occurred, which bond/currency/maturity was meant, and whether the measured outcome matched magnitude as well as direction. This is why the economic ledger records overstatements and misses rather than counting every directional resemblance as a hit.

Proclamation is not the same thing as prediction

The October 1 public corpus makes this distinction necessary. A sentence such as “I am God”, a rebuke such as “deaf … and blind”, or a public warning to an audience can resemble biblical prophetic speech without predicting a later event. In this archive, that class is prophetic proclamation / commission-form: the evidentiary question is whether the wording, audience, repetition, refusal and consequences are dated—not whether a future target resolved.

Public silence is not assent. Non-protest can be recorded as reception history, but it does not settle the truth of a theological claim.

Forecasting needs a stricter contract than prophecy language

If a claim is going to count as a forecast rather than a symbolic precursor, the archive should record target, horizon, resolution rule, prior probability/confidence, baseline and what would count as a miss. A later update should append to the forecast history rather than overwriting the earlier probability.

Question. What exactly is being predicted?
Target. Which event or variable resolves the claim?
Horizon. By when?
Resolution. Which source/rule decides the outcome?
Probability. How likely did the forecaster think it was before resolution?
Baseline. What would an outside-view/base-rate estimate have been?
Miss. What outcome would count against the prediction?

One memorable hit is not forecasting skill

Forecasting research improves when forecasters make many resolvable probability judgments and keep score. A Brier score for a binary forecast is the squared distance between the stated probability and the eventual 0/1 outcome; lower scores are better. Calibration asks whether events assigned, say, 70% probability actually happen about 70% of the time over a sufficiently large set. That prevents one spectacular-looking match from carrying the entire evidentiary burden.

The practical archive consequence is simple: preserve a denominator. Hits, misses, unresolved questions, vague claims and conditional scenarios all remain visible.

Hindsight is an evidence problem, not just a memory problem

Experimental research repeatedly finds hindsight bias: once people know an outcome, it can feel more predictable than it did beforehand. Close replications published in 2021 found the effect in retrospective and prospective judgments, and a 2025 fifty-year review describes it across experimental and real-world settings. That is why this Room privileges timestamped prior wording over remembered certainty.

The archive should therefore never reconstruct “what Tim clearly meant beforehand” from a later interpretation if the pre-event record is weaker. Preserve the old uncertainty, alternatives and incomplete wording.

The resolution protocol

1. Recover the earliest exact wording and timestamp.
2. Define what would count as a match—and ideally what would not—before judging the later event.
3. Build the external event timeline from independent sources.
4. Separate literal match, structural match, symbolic match and temporal proximity.
5. Record mismatches explicitly.
6. Classify the result rather than forcing a binary fulfilled/failed answer.
7. Reserve “fulfilled prediction” for sufficiently prior and specific material.

A revelation can be backward-looking

Later synthesis often makes earlier events intelligible. That is a legitimate interpretive process. The mistake begins when the archive rewrites the earlier event as though the mature interpretation was already explicit at the time.

This is why April 2025, 2019/20, the Great Book and later Bible comparisons need separate timestamps for event, original wording and later recognition.

Prediction audits should preserve misses

The economic inflation ledger is a useful model because it records directional hits alongside factual errors and magnitude overstatements. A forecast archive becomes less credible if every ambiguous line is rehabilitated after the fact while misses disappear.

The same anti-cherry-picking rule applies across the project: do not split one broad sentence into several “successful predictions” after the event; do not merge failed alternatives into one later success; do not award credit for details that were not fixed beforehand; and keep directional accuracy separate from magnitude accuracy.

Prophecy can be conditional

“If X, then Y” should not be scored as a failed unconditional forecast if X never happened. Conversely, a question or scenario should not later be upgraded into a confident prediction merely because Y occurred. Claim type belongs in the record.

Biblical fulfillment language requires an extra boundary

A biblical pattern can illuminate a project event without establishing that the biblical author predicted Tim or the Potatoverse. The Bible Room owns textual context; this Room owns the timing and evidentiary status of any claimed fulfillment relation.
