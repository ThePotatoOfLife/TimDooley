Games & Simulations

A simulation forces an idea to pay rent. Once growth, inheritance, scarcity, thresholds, agents or attention become rules, the project can watch what those rules actually produce.

Simulation behavior is model output, not automatically a claim about the real world. A game can reveal the consequences of its own rules; external validity requires separate evidence.

The creative archive contains more than jokes with buttons. Several game ideas are miniature laboratories for recurring project questions: what does selection do across generations, what happens when time is compressed, how do audiences change a system by watching it, and when does a Door event create a genuinely new state?

Potato of Life Growth Game.

The tamagotchi-like growth concept combines watering, growth time, size, yield, genetics, mutations, money, research, weather, seasons, family trees and rare events. Its real analytical value is iteration: environment and inherited traits interact, selection has tradeoffs, and one generation becomes the starting condition of the next.

Self-Playing AI Potato Town.

A top-down autonomous town places Tim/God on a couch, Son at the Door and agents inside accelerated seasons where a minute can represent roughly a month. The game turns observer-versus-participant, compressed time, emergent narrative and threshold events into something visible.

Tim Dooley Trading Game.

The browser-market concept uses tickers such as oil, gold, silver and Bitcoin inside a market-style interface. It is not a data authority; it is a way to model price signals, attention, feedback and the stories people wrap around changing numbers.

A good simulation does not prove the worldview. It reveals what the worldview's rules would do if you let them move.

Dog House game-show satire.

The deliberately fictional surveillance/game-show premise uses voting, scarcity, spectacle and audience distance to make internet incentive systems grotesquely obvious. Its value is cultural critique, not real-world instruction.

Potato School turns learning into threshold play.

The recovered concept mixes cultivation, Doors, passwords, keys, puzzles and absurd initiation satire. Its best insight is that learning can be embodied as a quest while simultaneously mocking prestige and credential gatekeeping.

Potato of Life Growth Game

A generational cultivation simulation: grow potatoes, select offspring, sell some, reinvest and watch inherited traits interact with environment.

  • State: water, growth time, size, speed, yield, money, research
  • Variation: genetics, mutations, weather, seasons, rare events
  • Memory: family tree + generation counter
Tests: whether selection and environmental pressure produce interesting tradeoffs instead of one trivial “best potato.”
Recovered design · prototype status unresolved

Self-Playing AI Potato Town

A top-down autonomous town with Tim/God on a couch, Son at the Door and agents living through accelerated seasons.

  • Time: ~1 minute ≈ 1 in-world month
  • State: agents, economy, seasons, Door events
  • Mode: observer rather than direct control
Tests: emergence, observer/participant differences and whether small local rules generate recognizable larger stories.
Recovered design · data source explicitly external

Tim Dooley Trading Game

A market-style browser interface using oil, gold, silver and Bitcoin tickers with TTS and self-updating display logic.

  • Objects: tickers and price changes
  • Interface: market board + spoken updates
  • Boundary: not an economic-data authority
Tests: how price signals, attention and narrative feedback change what users notice and value.
Conversation archaeology · original artifact missing

Secret Hidden Esoteric Potato School

An initiation-game parody built from cultivation, Doors, keys, passwords, puzzles and deliberately absurd prestige/tuition jokes.

  • Progression: hidden Door → puzzle → key → assignment
  • Theme: grow the potato instead of merely consuming it
  • Tone: esoteric-school satire
Tests: whether philosophy becomes more memorable when taught as threshold play while the game mocks credential gatekeeping.
Fictional satire · no real-world instruction

Dog House Surveillance Game-Show

An intentionally grotesque audience/game-show system where voting, scarcity, spectacle and surveillance make incentive mechanics obvious.

  • Actors: subjects, audience, voting system
  • Mechanics: scarcity, task selection, observation
  • Boundary: fiction / cultural critique
Tests: how distance from the subject changes audience incentives and how gamification can normalize dehumanizing treatment.

A game is a confession written in rules

The moment you let somebody play your philosophy, the hidden assumptions begin answering back

In prose you can say “growth matters” and leave the word glowing. In a game you have to decide what growth costs, what counts as success, what resources are scarce, what can die, what can be inherited, what a player is allowed to ignore, and whether the fastest strategy also destroys the world that made winning possible.

That is why simulation is such a merciless friend. It catches the values you forgot to declare. If every optimal strategy produces one giant potato and a dead field, the rules have taught you something about the worldview whether or not the manifesto intended it.

RewardWhat does the system make easy to want?

Points, survival, money, prestige, knowledge, care, novelty and control create different players even when the interface looks the same.

CostWho or what pays for the strategy?

A game that hides maintenance, exhaustion or externalities can accidentally teach extraction as competence.

MemoryDoes history matter?

Inheritance, reputation, ecological damage and accumulated trust make yesterday part of today’s state rather than scenery.

ExitCan the player refuse the premise?

Sometimes the most interesting move is not winning better, but discovering that the rules themselves deserve revision.

A good game does not merely illustrate a philosophy. It gives the philosophy a body sturdy enough to trip over its own feet.

Worked miniature · inspect the rules

The harvest that wins three turns and loses five

This is a deliberately small invented model, not a simulation of real potato yields. It is here because a worldview that praises growth should state its reward horizon and resource assumptions before declaring a winning strategy.

Starting state: six soil units, zero points, five turns. Harvesting requires at least two soil units. Choose the same action on each turn. Extract spends two soil units and awards four points. Tend spends two soil units, restores those two in the same modeled turn, and awards three points. If fewer than two soil units remain, neither harvest can score. The replenishment rule is a modeling assumption—not free energy in a real field.

Extract · short-run leadTurn 3: 12 points. Turn 5: still 12.

Soil follows 6 → 4 → 2 → 0. Extracting produces 4 + 4 + 4 points and then cannot harvest on turns four and five. Its early lead was real; its missing future was real too.

Tend · slower early scoreTurn 3: 9 points. Turn 5: 15.

Soil stays at six under the stipulated renewal rule. Tending produces three points per turn. On turn three it appears to lose; by turn five it leads. Change the regeneration assumption and the result must be calculated again.

What this tests: whether an apparent victory survives a change of horizon. What it cannot establish: that tending always wins, that social growth behaves like soil, or that every ecological process follows these cartoon mechanics. The next game revision should let players change initial soil, extraction rate, recovery rate, and horizon, then display both score and remaining capacity. See Model Testing for falsifiers and Potatoism for the moral question the toy model makes visible.

ContributionIdeas become behavior

Games make hidden assumptions explicit by turning them into rules, state changes and consequences that can actually run.

BoundaryModel output is not world evidence

A compelling simulation shows what follows from its rules. It does not establish that the real world uses those rules without external evidence.
