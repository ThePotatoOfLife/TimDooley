Potatoism · practice

How to become a Potato

Potatoism can be studied as philosophy, myth and theology. It can also be practiced. At the practical root, it begins with only two principles.

1. Be simple.

Remove unnecessary complication. Keep what supports life, clarity and useful action.

2. Grow toward light.

Choose the direction that increases truth, clarity, understanding, nourishment, care and future growth.

Everything else can be discovered again from those two rules. That is important. Potatoism should not become a giant list of commandments. It should become more capable while remaining simple.

Begin where you actually are

Suppose the day begins in Mud

You wake with too many things open at once. One problem is real, three are imagined futures, two are arguments that are no longer happening, and one task has been delayed long enough to acquire shame around it. The easiest mistake is to call all of this “my life.” Mud teaches something smaller: name the condition without making the condition your identity.

Do not rush immediately toward a grand solution. Some problems become clearer when you stop touching them for an hour. Burial, in practice, can mean sleep, privacy, study, silence, a notebook closed to the audience, or simply refusing to perform an answer before it exists. Hidden work is not automatically avoidance. The question is whether something is becoming more capable underneath.

Then one possibility becomes visible. Not ten. One. The email you can actually send. The source you can actually inspect. The apology you can actually make. The room you can actually clean. Eye is not omniscience; it is the first viable direction that survives attention.

Now ask what must exist before the possibility can live tomorrow. That is Root. A calendar entry. A saved document. A boundary. A tool. A night's sleep. Someone else knowing the plan. Root is boring because support usually is. The invisible part of a change is often the part that decides whether the change survives motivation.

Only then does Sprout deserve visibility. The first outward action should be small enough to learn from. If it fails, the failure should teach you something rather than destroy the whole attempt. A sprout does not prove the tree; it proves that the conditions permitted emergence.

And eventually the question changes. At first you asked, “Can I get out of this?” Later you ask, “What does this growth produce?” That is Fruit. If the result is only a more elaborate identity—more titles, more language, more reasons you are special—the cycle has probably curved back toward self-description. If another person can eat from it, use it, learn from it, or become less dependent because of it, the growth has entered the world.

The sequence is not a ladder of worth. It is a way of refusing to demand Fruit from a Seed, visibility from Burial, or certainty before an Eye has even opened.

The growth cycle

Mud. See the condition you are actually in.
Burial. Some work needs privacy, rest, study or incubation.
Eye. Notice one viable possibility.
Root. Stabilize what will support future growth.
Sprout. Take the smallest useful step toward light.
Ladder. Turn repeated good actions into pathways and systems.
Tree. Let capability branch without losing its root.
Fruit. Ask what your growth now produces for other life.
Seed. Preserve and share what can begin another cycle.
Return. Begin again, simpler and more informed.

Efficiency appears naturally

If you are trying to be simple and you know which direction is light, wasted effort becomes easier to see. Efficiency is therefore not a third commandment so much as a discovery produced by the first two. The Potato asks: can this grow with less friction while preserving what matters?

Wu wei is a useful comparison

Potatoism can sometimes resemble the Daoist idea of wu wei: action that is less forced and better aligned with the conditions already present. The Potatoist version is simple: find the viable direction already available, then help it grow rather than exhausting yourself by fighting everything at once.

This is a comparison, not a claim that Daoism and Potatoism are historically the same tradition.

Carve light into the dark

Growing toward light does not always mean leaving difficult conditions behind. A root grows through soil. A sprout creates a path. Knowledge, care, repair, beauty and relationship can do the same thing in human life. The practical question becomes: what small channel of light can exist here that did not exist before?

Fruit is the test

A Potato is not valuable merely because it survives. It nourishes. So growth should eventually produce something beyond self-description: clearer understanding, useful work, care, repair, art, tools, better relationships, protection, food, opportunity or conditions in which more life can grow.

The fruit test: if a claimed spiritual development creates only more titles, more complexity or more self-importance, simplify again. If it creates useful capacity, nourishment and better conditions for life, the growth has somewhere to go.

Potatoism is iterative

The cycle is not completed once. Each iteration can reveal something new. A person may discover efficiency, then relationship, then responsibility, then cultivation, then another form nobody has named yet. But every new discovery must survive the return to the root:

Does it help us be simpler? Does it help us grow toward light?

If not, it may be interesting mythology without being a core practice.

Advanced symbolic forms

Later Potatoism can describe higher modes symbolically: the Gardener cultivates conditions for other life; an angelic mode joins capability with care, truthfulness and service; a Valkyrie mode can symbolize courage, discernment and carrying hard-earned wisdom forward. These are aspirations and symbolic comparisons, not requirements for becoming a Potato.

A seven-line daily Potato practice

1. Simplify one thing.
2. Identify one source of light.
3. Take one small useful step toward it.
4. Remove one unnecessary friction.
5. Help one thing beyond yourself grow.
6. Notice what actually happened.
7. Keep the lesson, simplify, and begin again.
