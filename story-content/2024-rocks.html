You Do Not Need to Carry Them

A fresh young potato finds the Sage in the garden and asks how to grow. Tim gives it soil, simplicity and patience. Then the potato asks what happens if people keep handing it more rocks.

Hear the full story

It is early morning in the Great Book. The moon is going down, the sun is beginning to come up, and a young potato wanders across the garden looking for somebody who can explain what growing is supposed to feel like. In the distance Tim is already working the soil.

The potato approaches carefully and asks the question as plainly as it can: how do I grow, and how do I become like you?

Tim does not give it a ladder, a title or a secret. He sits on a rock and talks about slowness. A potato grows under the surface before anybody applauds it. It uses what is around it. Soil is not an embarrassment; soil is the condition that makes growth possible.

The younger potato is not convinced that simplicity will survive contact with the world. There is noise everywhere. Other people want things. Other people misunderstand. Tim tells it not to let somebody else decide what soil it belongs in.

Then he picks up a rock.

He turns it in his hand and gives it to the potato as an image of unnecessary complexity. One rock is manageable. A life built around carrying every rock handed to you is something else. The young potato looks at it, puts it back down, and asks the question that changes the scene: what if people keep giving me more?

Tim stands up and answers, “You do not need to carry them.”

The rest of the lesson follows from that. Roots can choose direction. Anger and fear can become information without becoming masters. A crow lands nearby. Tim tells the potato that tests will keep arriving, but the soil will still be there.

At the end, the young potato does the least spectacular thing possible: it plants itself. Tim returns to his work. The lesson ends in dirt rather than revelation.

That line becomes more interesting because the later Story will repeatedly show Tim carrying things anyway—people, hours, arguments, grief, obligations, whole symbolic worlds. In the Great Book, the adviser already knows the sentence. The later life keeps testing whether he can live it.
