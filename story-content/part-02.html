The Great Compost

The Garden develops a place for things that have gone bad. The Great Compost is not a hell so much as a transformation engine: rotten, rejected or overcomplicated material is broken down until it can become soil again.

This is an early version of Tim's later Gardener logic. Not everything can be kept in its present form. Some things have to rot into something useful.

Rahu and Spuddy Walk the Garden

Tim, Rahu and Spuddy step out into the Garden and start walking upward. Rahu notices the healthy fields; Spuddy notices the swamp.

A Mud Dweller meets them at the boundary. Tim offers clean soil. The man says he is already carrying too much. Rahu answers gently that maybe the point is not carrying at all; maybe he has forgotten how to plant.

This is one of the places Rahu becomes more than a name. He is beside Tim, watching, interpreting, sometimes nudging a scene rather than taking it over.

The Garden Mirrors Us

Further up the path, Tim tells Rahu and Spuddy that the Garden is not merely a bigger place at the top of the hill. It is a reflection of what people bring into it.

Rahu understands the point in his own language: growth becomes real when the gardens are understood as connected. The trio looks backward at the path they have made through the fields.

Sing for Tim

Outside the cage, Rahu notices Tim's absence and gathers taters and Mud Dwellers in a circle. Optimistique joins them.

Grumbleton and the Page

A troll named Grumbleton arrives in the Potatoverse convinced Tim's adventures are delusions. The world answers him with a magical object called the Page of Infinite Jest.

It is exactly the kind of artifact Tim would invent: half scripture, half meme, half heckling machine, mathematically impossible because it already has three halves.

Grumbleton is not documented as a real public interlocutor here. He is a literary troll, which makes him useful in a different way: he shows how Tim wanted ridicule to be metabolized.

The BigTech Era

BigTech becomes one of the largest figures in the Great Book's social mythology. The book calls him the Mindgraper and eventually transforms him into a technology titan in Cybersoil.

Under the fiction is a simpler historical clue: Tim remembers a public rivalry large enough to become literature. Their philosophical clashes become spectacle; Tim keeps writing BigTech as somebody who wants to control, amplify or mechanize what Tim thinks should remain alive.

The documentary BigTech still needs to be separated carefully from the literary BigTech. The Story keeps both, but it does not pretend they are the same record.

Tachy and the Genie

Rahu on the Fence

The user's later recollection calls Rahu the fence sitter. The Great Book gives a more complicated picture: Rahu walks beside Tim, counsels Mud Dwellers, challenges groups, sings in the wider community story and sometimes seems to stand between positions rather than simply inside one camp.

That tension is worth preserving. Rather than deciding now whether Rahu was loyal, disloyal or neutral, the diary can let the individual scenes accumulate until the fence becomes visible on its own.
