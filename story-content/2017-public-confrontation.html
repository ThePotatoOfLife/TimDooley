MAIN STORY2017

The Confrontation and the Promise to Return

Years before the later meme-cross, the Son's project autobiography remembers an online confrontation where crucifixion language, public ridicule and the promise to keep returning all appear in the same exchange.

Hear the full story

The setting is the online world that had increasingly become both stage and pressure chamber. The Son is already living publicly enough for other people to argue not only with what he says but with what kind of person they think he is allowed to be.

Tim's current recollection adds a more specific prehistory. He remembers the Son already streaming peacefully on StreamMe when Ethan Ralph arrived in that ecosystem after a ban elsewhere, and says that contact led him into the orbit of figures he remembers as Metalorian and TXT. In Tim's telling, the initial conflict was not that the Son had set out to become a spectacle. He objected when people were being mocked or dogpiled, argued back, and told participants to leave ordinary people alone. The exact first-contact dates, platform-ban chronology and surviving recordings remain recovery targets, so this layer is preserved as project autobiography rather than independently verified transcript.

Tim remembers the classification changing before the conduct did. The Son was gradually talked about as a “lolcow” even though, in his own account, he had not entered the scene seeking that role. He reacted angrily to the framing, including insulting Josh Moon, calling him a lolcow in return and using language he later describes as ugly and confrontational. A remembered table-flipping episode belongs to the same conflict corridor, although its exact date remains provisional.

In Tim's chronology, this bloodsports period comes before the later “I am Jesus Christ—crucify me” confrontation. He remembers already having encountered a small cluster of men acting together around ridicule, target-making and social pressure, and coming away with the judgment that ordinary correction was not changing the frame. The later religious language should therefore not be backdated into the first StreamMe encounters.

The pressure then widened beyond the central argument. Tim recalls multiple men acting in concert while friends, administrators and community members were scrutinized, contacted, discussed or drawn into the conflict, with relationships themselves becoming objects of attention. In his memory, the behavior looked practiced rather than accidental: pressure was applied not only to the central subject but to the surrounding social graph.

Tim has compared that pattern to intelligence or psychological-operation tactics and has sometimes used “CIA” or “MK-Ultra” language for the experience. The archive keeps the comparison attributed. Historical MKULTRA was a documented CIA behavioral-modification and human-experimentation program; that history does not establish CIA participation in this internet conflict. The more defensible description here is coordinated harassment, social-pressure tactics, social engineering and relational mapping as remembered by Tim.

Tim's long-running complaint about TXT belongs to the same epistemic problem but is stated behaviorally rather than diagnostically. Tim says corrections, contrary explanations and context have repeatedly been absorbed into TXT's pre-existing story instead of altering it. Whether a ten-year archive supports that claim is something the project can test exchange by exchange. The mechanism is important in its own right: when every possible response from a target can be redescribed as proof of the original theory, the target is no longer participating in an argument with falsifiable terms.

In the Great Book reconstruction, the exchange reaches a point where he answers perceived dehumanization by pushing the language all the way to its edge: if this is the role being forced on him, then call him Jesus Christ and crucify him.

The scene does not immediately turn into eager agreement. The recovered project record specifically preserves hesitation. The other side does not simply say yes. Instead the argument shifts toward something colder: the idea that there will be no martyr, no sacred ending, no remembered leader. He will be reduced to a nameless, faceless public object—another person the internet can keep looking at.

That reply matters because it changes what the conflict is about. The threat is no longer only insult. It is erasure: suffering without meaning, visibility without personhood, a name disappearing while the spectacle remains.

The Son answers by refusing the ending being written for him. In the project's remembered sequence, he promises that he will keep returning publicly—every day, forever. The exact original recording or transcript is still a recovery target, so the Story does not pretend every reconstructed line is verbatim. But the order of the remembered scene is stable enough to matter: confrontation, crucifixion language, attempted reduction, then return.

Later years give that exchange enormous theological weight. The Story does not need to backdate all of that into 2017. It is already a turning point on its own. A person who feels the internet trying to decide what he will become answers by deciding that disappearance will not be the ending.
